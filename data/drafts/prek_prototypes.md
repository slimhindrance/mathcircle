# Pre-K prototypes — first 5 problems

**Purpose:** Test the agreed Pre-K problem shape on real Pre-K kids before authoring
at scale. Iterate at the kitchen table, then port the surviving shape into the
problem schema + UI.

**Shape we're testing:**

- **Parent-only screen.** Parent reads the card on the app; kid never looks at the device.
- **Kitchen-table, manipulative-led.** Materials are everyday objects (fingers, blocks,
  pebbles, cereal, cups, paper, crayons). No printable required.
- **5–8 minutes.** Pre-K attention budget is short. One problem per session is fine.
- **One of the four magic prompts is the heart of the interaction.** The parent's job
  is mostly to ask the prompt well and *wait*.
- **Optional "what did your kid say?" capture afterward.** Voluntary; this is the
  corpus we'll mine for product improvement later.

Each problem below carries the fields we'll eventually formalize into the schema:
`title`, `strand`, `level` (1 = Pre-K), `materials`, `parent_script`, `magic_prompt`,
`what_to_listen_for`, `capture_field`.

## 1. How many? (subitizing — number sense)

- **Strand:** number_sense
- **Level:** 1 (Pre-K)
- **Materials:** 4–5 small identical objects — pebbles, raisins, blocks, pennies.
- **Time:** 3–5 min.

### Parent script

> Sit across from your child at the table. Put two objects on the table, side
> by side. Cover them with your hand for a second, then lift your hand and
> immediately put it back down — your child sees the objects for about one
> second. Ask: **"How many?"**
>
> Let them answer. Don't react yet. Then put three objects out and do the same
> thing. Then one. Then four. Each time, just **"how many?"** — and wait.

### Magic prompt

> After they've answered for two or three of these, ask: **"How did you know?"**
>
> The point is not the right number. The point is whether they *saw* it (subitizing)
> or *counted* it. Both are good; we just want them to start noticing the difference.

### What to listen for

- "I just knew" → they're subitizing. Strong sign.
- "One, two, three" (in the air with a finger) → they're counting. Also good — slower
  but accurate.
- A wrong number → no correction. Try again with the same arrangement and ask "let's
  look together — how many?"

### Optional: what did your kid say?

> (Free text — what did they actually say? What did they do with their fingers or
> eyes? This helps us understand how Pre-K kids approach quantity.)

## 2. One for me, one for you (one-to-one correspondence — number sense)

- **Strand:** number_sense
- **Level:** 1 (Pre-K)
- **Materials:** 4–6 small cups (or bowls, or paper squares) and the same number of
  spoons (or pebbles, or coins). One "extra" pair on the side.
- **Time:** 5–7 min.

### Parent script

> Put 4 cups in a row in front of your child. Put a pile of spoons next to them.
> Say: **"Can you give each cup exactly one spoon?"**
>
> Watch them work. Don't help unless they ask. When they're done — whether they got
> it right or not — ask: **"Are there enough? How do you know?"**

### Magic prompt

> **"How do you know?"** — let them show you. Some kids will physically lift a
> spoon over each cup ("this one, this one, this one"). Some will count the cups,
> then count the spoons. Some will just look. All of these are valid; you're
> learning *how they think*, not catching mistakes.

### What to listen for

- Physical one-to-one matching (a spoon hovering above each cup, going one by one)
  is the foundation skill we're after. This is more important than counting at this
  age.
- If they pair them off correctly but can't explain why, that's fine — the
  understanding is in the doing.
- **Variation if it's too easy:** add an extra spoon to the pile. "Is there a spoon
  for every cup?" Now they have to notice the leftover.

### Optional: what did your kid say?

> (Free text — what did they do *physically*? Did they pair them by lifting? By
> counting? Did they look puzzled at any point?)

## 3. Red, blue, red, blue... (repeating patterns — patterns)

- **Strand:** patterns
- **Level:** 1 (Pre-K)
- **Materials:** Anything that comes in two colors — blocks, buttons, two types of
  cereal, red and green grapes, crayons. ~8 of each color.
- **Time:** 5 min.

### Parent script

> Build a pattern on the table with your child watching: **red, blue, red, blue,
> red, blue.** Then point past the last one and ask: **"What comes next?"**
>
> Whatever they say, smile and place it. Then continue: **"And after that?"**
>
> Keep going until they're confident. Then build a different one — **blue, blue,
> red, blue, blue, red** — and start over.

### Magic prompt

> Once they've extended a pattern a few times, ask: **"What did you notice?"**
> or **"What's the rule?"**
>
> The leap from "I know what comes next" to "I can say what the pattern *is*" is
> a big developmental moment. Many Pre-K kids can do the first without the second
> — and that's okay. The articulation is what we're nurturing.

### What to listen for

- They predict correctly but can't articulate the rule → developmentally typical.
  Just say "yes, red-blue-red-blue, you saw it!"
- They invent a wrong rule that's internally consistent ("it's red-blue-red-blue-red")
  → fascinating. Let them follow it and see what happens.
- They say "I don't know" → no problem. Build the pattern with them. The doing
  *is* the noticing at this age.

### Optional: what did your kid say?

> (Free text — did they predict the next piece without thinking, or pause to look?
> Did they say a "rule" in their own words?)

## 4. Which one doesn't belong? (attribute reasoning — logic & sorting)

- **Strand:** logic_classification
- **Level:** 1 (Pre-K)
- **Materials:** 4 objects from around the house. Use a set where multiple "doesn't
  belong" answers are defensible. **Good set example:** 3 spoons + a fork. Or: a
  yellow ball, a yellow lemon, a yellow rubber duck, a green apple. Or: 3 round
  things + 1 square thing.
- **Time:** 5 min.

### Parent script

> Lay the 4 objects out where your child can see them. Ask: **"Which one doesn't
> belong?"**
>
> Whatever they pick, don't say right or wrong. Ask: **"Why?"**

### Magic prompt

> After they've answered, ask: **"Can you find a different one that doesn't belong,
> for a different reason?"**
>
> This is the magic of the problem: there is no single right answer. Every object
> can be "the odd one out" for some reason. We are training the muscle of
> *looking again with new eyes.*

### What to listen for

- "It's the only X" → they've identified an attribute (color, shape, size, function).
  Excellent.
- They flip and pick a different one for a different reason → you've just watched
  abstract reasoning happen.
- They get stuck on one answer ("it has to be the fork because it has pointy ends") →
  totally fine. Try a different set tomorrow and see.

### Optional: what did your kid say?

> (Free text — what reasons did they give? Did the second reason surprise you? Did
> they invent a category you wouldn't have thought of?)

## 5. Where could they sit? (counting ways — counting ways)

- **Strand:** counting_ways
- **Level:** 1 (Pre-K)
- **Materials:** 2 small stuffed animals (or toy cars, or plastic figures) and 2
  plates (or boxes, or paper squares marked "seat 1" and "seat 2"). Optional: a
  third animal for the extension.
- **Time:** 5–7 min.

### Parent script

> Put two plates in front of your child and say: **"Look, two seats at the dinner
> table. And here are our two friends — Bear and Bunny. Where can each one sit?
> Can you find all the ways?"**
>
> Let them physically place the animals. When they find one arrangement, say
> **"Great! Is there a different way?"** and have them swap.

### Magic prompt

> Once they've found both ways (Bear in seat 1, Bunny in seat 2; then swapped),
> ask: **"What if we had three friends — Bear, Bunny, and Lion? How many ways
> then?"**
>
> Don't expect them to solve it. The goal is the *what if* leap — the smallest
> version of mathematical conjecture. They'll probably want to try with three
> animals and three seats. Let them; that's the whole point.

### What to listen for

- They find the two arrangements but think they're the same → "look, in this one
  Bear is on the left, in this one Bear is on the right. Are those the same or
  different?"
- They try the 3-animal case and *immediately* see there are more ways → exceptional.
  Note this.
- They lose interest after the 2-animal case → fine. The "what if" lives in their
  head for next time.

### Optional: what did your kid say?

> (Free text — did they treat the swapped arrangement as a new way, or the same?
> Did the 3-animal extension excite them or overwhelm them?)

# Test plan

Before authoring more at scale, test these 5 on a Pre-K kid you can observe
directly (Mila is K but recent enough that she's the closest target; failing that
a friend's 3- or 4-year-old). For each:

1. **Did the parent script land?** Did you find yourself going off-script in ways
   the script should have anticipated?
2. **Did the magic prompt actually get asked, and did the kid respond to it?** Or
   did the kid bail before the prompt?
3. **Was 5–8 min the right time budget?** Too long? Too short?
4. **Was the "what did your kid say?" capture worth filling out?** Did it feel
   like effort or like reflection?
5. **Would you tell another parent about this one?** (The shareability test.)

Survivors of those questions become the shape for the next batch. Casualties get
rewritten or cut.
