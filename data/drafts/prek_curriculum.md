# Pre-K curriculum — first complete draft

**62 problems** across 10 strands, all `level: 1` (Pre-K). Distribution:

| Strand | Count | Why this weight at Pre-K |
|---|---:|---|
| number_sense | 10 | Foundational — subitizing, counting, one-to-one |
| patterns | 8 | Developmentally strong; rich at this age |
| logic_classification | 8 | Attribute games are Pre-K natural territory |
| geometry_spatial | 8 | Shape, symmetry, position — kitchen-table friendly |
| measurement | 6 | Comparison ("longer", "heavier") not units |
| math_games | 6 | Simple turn-taking, early strategy |
| add_sub_structures | 5 | "One more / one less" intuition, not formal ops |
| equality_balance | 4 | Same/different with objects, not symbols |
| counting_ways | 4 | Combinatorial intuition with 2–3 objects |
| missing_number_stories | 3 | Hide-and-reveal games, the seed of algebra |

**Format (every problem):**

- **Strand / Level / Materials / Time**
- **Parent script** — what the parent reads and does. Naturalistic, no jargon.
- **Magic prompt** — one of the four (how/why, another way, what if, what did you notice), specific to the problem.
- **What to listen for** — real Pre-K behaviors, not idealized ones.
- **Optional: what did your kid say?** — voluntary capture, asking for specifics we want to mine later.

**Universal Pre-K rules baked into every problem:**

- Parent-only screen. Kid never looks at the device.
- Materials are everyday: fingers, blocks, raisins, cups, paper, crayons, stuffed animals, coins.
- 3–8 minutes per problem. One problem per session is plenty.
- No "wrong" answers in the parent's voice. We're observing, not correcting.

# Number Sense (10)

## 1. How many? (subitizing 1–4)

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 4–5 small identical objects (pebbles, raisins, blocks, pennies) · **Time:** 3–5 min

### Parent script
> Sit across from your child at the table. Put two objects out, side by side. Cover with your hand for a second, lift and immediately put your hand back down — your child sees them for about one second. Ask: **"How many?"** Repeat with 3, then 1, then 4 objects, each time covering between rounds.

### Magic prompt
> After 2–3 rounds, ask: **"How did you know?"** The point is not the right number — it's whether they *saw it* (subitizing) or *counted it*. Both are good.

### What to listen for
- "I just knew" → subitizing. Strong sign at this age.
- Counting in the air with a finger → also good, slower but accurate.
- A wrong number → no correction. Try again with the same arrangement.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> What did they actually say? What did they do with their fingers or eyes?

## 2. One for me, one for you (one-to-one correspondence)

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 4–6 cups + 4–6 spoons · **Time:** 5–7 min

### Parent script
> Put 4 cups in a row. Put a pile of spoons next to them. Say: **"Can you give each cup exactly one spoon?"** Watch them work. Don't help unless they ask.

### Magic prompt
> When they're done: **"Are there enough? How do you know?"** Some kids will physically pair (lifting a spoon over each cup), some will count cups then spoons, some will just look. All valid.

### What to listen for
- Physical one-to-one matching is the foundation skill — more important than counting at this age.
- If they pair correctly but can't explain why, that's fine. The understanding is in the doing.
- Variation: add an extra spoon. "Is there one for every cup?" Now they have to notice the leftover.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they pair physically or count? Did they notice the extra?

## 3. Count up to the cookie

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 5 raisins (or any small treat your kid likes), one plate · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Put 5 raisins on a plate. Tell your child: **"You can eat them all. But first, count them with me. One … two … three … four … five."** Touch each one as you both say the number. Then say: **"Now you count, and I'll touch."**

### Magic prompt
> After they've counted, ask: **"How many will be left if we eat one?"** Eat one (or let them eat one). Recount together. "How many now?"

### What to listen for
- Are they pointing/touching one item per number word (one-to-one with the count)?
- Do they say "four" when there are four, or are they reciting numbers without tracking?
- Recounting after eating one — do they know they have to recount, or just say "one less"?

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they get the count right? Did they need to recount or did they know "one less"?

## 4. More or less?

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 8–10 small objects (cereal, coins, blocks) · **Time:** 3–5 min

### Parent script
> Make two piles on the table: one pile of 3, one pile of 6. Ask: **"Which pile has more?"** They'll point. Now make piles of 4 and 5. Ask again. Then make 7 and 2.

### Magic prompt
> **"How do you know which has more?"** Some kids will say "it looks bigger." Some will count both piles. Some will pair them off one-to-one. Each is a different way of comparing.

### What to listen for
- Visual judgment (looks bigger) works for very different sizes but breaks down for close ones — let them discover this.
- Counting both piles is more advanced.
- One-to-one pairing (lining them up) is the most mature strategy.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Which strategy did they use? Did it change when the piles were close in size?

## 5. Hidden hands

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** Just your hands · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Hold up 3 fingers behind your back. Bring them out for one second, then hide again. Ask: **"How many fingers?"** Repeat with 2, 4, 1, 5. Mix one and two hands.

### Magic prompt
> **"How did you see it so fast?"** This is subitizing on fingers — pre-cursor to instant number sense. By Pre-K end, most kids should subitize 1–4 reliably.

### What to listen for
- Speed matters here — instant answers indicate subitizing.
- 1–3 fingers should be instant; 4–5 is still developing at this age.
- If they want to count slowly, fine — show fewer fingers so they can succeed at subitizing.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Which numbers were instant? Which needed counting?

## 6. The fingers game

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** Just hands · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Say a number from 1 to 5. Your child holds up that many fingers. Then they say a number and you hold up that many. Take turns.

### Magic prompt
> Pick numbers where they have to *think* — start with 1, 2, 3 (easy) then jump to 4 or 5. After they show 5, ask: **"Can you show 5 a different way?"** Most kids will show one whole hand. Can they show 3+2? 4+1?

### What to listen for
- Decomposing 5 into 4+1 or 3+2 is a big developmental moment — usually late Pre-K or K.
- If they always go all-one-hand-then-one, that's developmentally fine; the alternative will come.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they find different ways to show 5? What were they?

## 7. Build me a tower of 3

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 10+ blocks (any kind) · **Time:** 3–5 min

### Parent script
> Say: **"Can you build me a tower of three blocks?"** Watch them build. When done, ask: **"How tall is it?"** Then: **"Can you make one that's four blocks tall?"**

### Magic prompt
> After they build several, ask: **"Which tower is tallest? How do you know?"** They can compare by counting or by looking. Both are fine.

### What to listen for
- Do they count as they stack, or stack and then count?
- Do they overshoot (build 5 when asked for 3) and self-correct?
- Comparing two towers — visual or counted?

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they count while building or after? Any self-correction?

## 8. Empty cup, full cup

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** A cup, water or dry cereal · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Show your child a cup. Pour cereal in until it's full. Say: **"This is full."** Pour it back out. **"This is empty."** Now pour halfway. **"What's this?"** They might say "some" or "half."

### Magic prompt
> **"Can you show me a cup with a little bit? With a lot? With nothing?"** This is the conceptual cousin of zero/few/many before number words attach.

### What to listen for
- Vocabulary: full, empty, some, little, lot — at Pre-K these are the building blocks of comparison.
- "Half" is sophisticated for Pre-K; many won't have the word but will pour to the right level.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> What words did they use for "some" or "half"? Did the language surprise you?

## 9. The cookie monster game (counting backwards)

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 5 small cookies/crackers/coins, one plate · **Time:** 3–5 min

### Parent script
> Put 5 items on a plate. Say: **"There are five cookies. The cookie monster is hungry."** Use your hand as a puppet (chomp sound) and "eat" one. **"How many now?"** Continue: 4, 3, 2, 1, 0.

### Magic prompt
> After "zero," ask: **"What happens next? Can the monster keep eating?"** This is the conceptual seed of "you can't take from nothing" — but kids will sometimes invent fun answers ("the plate is the next cookie!"), which is great.

### What to listen for
- Counting backwards is harder than counting forwards. Stumbling is normal.
- "Zero" / "none" / "all gone" — what word do they reach for?
- Reaching the "nothing left" moment — do they understand it ended, or want to continue?

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> What did they call "zero"? What did they say when there were none?

## 10. Count the steps

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** Stairs, or just floor space · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Walk with your child from the kitchen to the front door (or up the stairs). Count each step out loud together: "One … two … three …" Match the count to the step.

### Magic prompt
> When you arrive, ask: **"How many steps was that?"** Then walk back and count again. **"Is it the same number? Why?"**

### What to listen for
- Counting with motion is harder than counting still objects — kids often skip or double.
- Realizing the count is the same both directions is a small but real "conservation" moment.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they keep their count synced with their feet? What did they say when the count came out different the second time?

# Add & Subtract Structures (5)

## 11. One more bear

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 5 small toys (animals, cars, dolls) · **Time:** 3–4 min

### Parent script
> Line up 2 bears. **"How many bears?"** They say "two." Add one more. **"And now? One more bear — how many?"** Continue: 3, then 4, then 5.

### Magic prompt
> **"How do you know it's three now?"** Some kids will recount the whole line. Some will just say "three" because they tracked the +1. Both are good.

### What to listen for
- Recounting after each addition → developmentally typical, building the count.
- Knowing "one more makes three" without recounting → starting to understand the *structure* of adding.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they recount each time, or know one more?

---

## 12. Hide and find

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 5 small objects, a cloth or towel · **Time:** 3–4 min

### Parent script
> Put 5 objects on the table. Have your child count them. Cover with a cloth. Reach under the cloth, take one away, hide it in your hand. Reveal: **"How many now?"** They count: 4. **"What happened?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"How do you know one is missing?"** The answer can be "I counted" or "I remember there were five." Either is great.

### What to listen for
- Recounting reveals current count but not necessarily the change.
- Saying "you took one" reveals tracking of the action.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they figure out one was missing by recounting or by memory of the original?

---

## 13. The sharing problem

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 4 raisins (or pennies, or crackers), 2 plates · **Time:** 3–5 min

### Parent script
> Put 2 plates out. Give your child 4 raisins. **"Can you share these so each plate has the same?"** Let them work.

### Magic prompt
> **"How do you know it's fair?"** They might say "I gave one to each" or "two and two." Both reveal different understanding.

### What to listen for
- Alternating distribution ("one for you, one for me") — the foundation of fair sharing.
- All-on-one-side first, then noticing it's unfair → also great, self-correcting is sophisticated.
- 6 items between 3 plates is the natural next step if this is easy.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> How did they distribute? Did they count after, or just check by looking?

---

## 14. Both hands together

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** Just hands · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Hold up 2 fingers on one hand. **"How many?"** Then hold up 3 on the other. **"And here?"** Now put both hands together. **"All together — how many?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"How did you find it? Did you count all of them, or count from where we were?"** "Count on" (saying "two … three, four, five") is more advanced than starting over.

### What to listen for
- Starting over and counting all 5 → developmentally typical Pre-K.
- Saying "two … three, four, five" — counting on — is early K territory and a meaningful leap.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they count all from one, or count on from the first number?

---

## 15. The empty bowl

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 4 objects, 1 bowl · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Put 4 objects in a bowl. **"How many in the bowl?"** Now take them all out, one by one, counting down: "three … two … one … zero." Say: **"What's in the bowl now?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"What's zero? Can we have less than zero things in the bowl?"** This question doesn't have a Pre-K-friendly answer (it does, but not without negative numbers) — the *asking* is what matters. Kids invent wonderful answers.

### What to listen for
- The word "zero" / "none" / "nothing" — which do they reach for?
- Their answer to "less than zero" is rarely correct mathematically, but it's a window into how they think about emptiness.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> What was their answer to "less than zero"? Worth saving verbatim.

# Missing Number Stories (3)

## 16. What's under the cup?

- **Strand:** missing_number_stories · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 2 opaque cups, 4 small objects (pennies, pebbles, raisins) · **Time:** 4–5 min

### Parent script
> Show your child 4 objects. Have them count: 4. While they watch, put 2 under one cup and 2 under the other. Lift one cup: 2 under here. **"How many under the other cup? How do you know?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"How did you figure it out?"** This is the missing-addend idea in disguise. 4 total, 2 visible — kid figures the other must be 2.

### What to listen for
- "Because four take away two is two" — verbal arithmetic. Late Pre-K.
- "Because they were the same when you split them" — structural memory. Excellent.
- A guess → fine. Reveal both cups. Try again with 5 objects (3+2 or 4+1).

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they reason or guess? If they reasoned — what was the reason?

---

## 17. The penny vanished

- **Strand:** missing_number_stories · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 3 pennies (or any small items), a cloth · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Show 3 pennies. Count them with your child: 3. Cover with cloth. While they watch, reach under, palm one (don't reveal it). Lift cloth: **"How many?"** They count: 2. **"What happened?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"Where is the third penny? Can you guess?"** This is a missing-quantity story in narrative form.

### What to listen for
- "You took it" (with confidence) → tracking the action.
- A bewildered shrug → fine. Reveal the penny in your hand and laugh.
- Older or quicker kids may want to play the trick on you next.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they figure out you palmed it? What was their guess?

---

## 18. Some animals went home

- **Strand:** missing_number_stories · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 5 small animal toys · **Time:** 3–4 min

### Parent script
> Line up 5 animals. **"Five animals at the park."** Make a story: **"Two went home for dinner."** Move 2 away. **"How many are still at the park?"** They count: 3.

### Magic prompt
> Repeat with different start numbers. Then reverse: **"There are 3 at the park. Some went home. How many went home?"** (Start with 5, end with 3, missing addend = 2.) This is harder — they may need to act it out.

### What to listen for
- Forward stories (start 5, take away 2, find 3) are accessible.
- Backward stories (3 left, find how many went) are the seed of algebra and may be too hard. Acting it out is the bridge.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did the backward story make sense? What did they say when stuck?

# Equality & Balance (4)

## 19. Same or different?

- **Strand:** equality_balance · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 6–8 small objects (pennies, beads) · **Time:** 3–4 min

### Parent script
> Make two small piles in front of your child: 3 pennies on the left, 3 pennies on the right. **"Are these the same or different?"** They say "same." Now make 3 on the left and 4 on the right. **"And now?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"How do you know?"** Counting both is one answer. Pairing them off (one on left next to one on right) is another. Either works.

### What to listen for
- Counting each pile separately → mature.
- Pairing physically → also mature, possibly more intuitive at this age.
- Just visual judgment → fine when piles are very different sizes; will fail when they're close.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> How did they check? Did they count or pair?

## 20. Make them match

- **Strand:** equality_balance · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 8–10 small objects · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Put 4 objects in front of you. **"I have four. Can you make a pile that's the same as mine?"** Let them work. When done, ask: **"Is yours the same as mine? How do you know?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"Can you show me, without counting, that they're the same?"** Pair them off. This is the foundation of equality-as-relation rather than equality-as-computed-result.

### What to listen for
- Counting their pile and stopping at 4 → counting strategy.
- Putting one of theirs next to each of yours → pairing strategy. Same answer, different concept.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Counting or pairing? Did they trust their answer?

---

## 21. Fair share

- **Strand:** equality_balance · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 6 raisins, 2 plates · **Time:** 3–4 min

### Parent script
> Give your child 6 raisins and 2 plates. **"Share them fairly between the two plates. Each plate gets the same."** Let them work.

### Magic prompt
> When done, ask: **"How do you know it's fair?"** Counting each plate, or pairing the plates by lifting one raisin from each — both are valid.

### What to listen for
- Alternating distribution — strong.
- Counting after distributing to check — also strong.
- Try 8 raisins next, then 5 (uneven). What do they do with the leftover?

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> How did they handle the leftover with 5 raisins?

## 22. The scale game

- **Strand:** equality_balance · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** A coat hanger + 2 paper cups with string (a homemade balance scale) OR a real kitchen scale · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Show them the scale. Put a heavy object in one side (a small can) and a light object (a feather, a paper square) in the other. **"Which side is heavier? How do you know?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"Can you make the two sides the same?"** They'll have to add more light objects until the scale balances — a physical equality.

### What to listen for
- They understand heavier = lower side / lighter = higher side.
- They iterate: add objects, observe, add more. This is the foundation of "balance both sides."
- The leap from "heavier" to "equal" is the equality concept in physical form.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they figure out how to balance it? How many tries?

# Patterns (8)

## 23. Red, blue, red, blue (color)

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 8 blocks or buttons in two colors, or two types of cereal · **Time:** 4–5 min

### Parent script
> Build a pattern: **red, blue, red, blue, red, blue.** Point past the last one and ask: **"What comes next?"** Keep going as long as they're engaged. Then build a different pattern (blue-blue-red, blue-blue-red) and start over.

### Magic prompt
> **"What did you notice?"** or **"What's the rule?"** The leap from predicting the next piece to articulating the rule is a big developmental step.

### What to listen for
- Correct predictions, no articulation → very common at Pre-K. The pattern is felt, not yet spoken.
- A wrong rule that's internally consistent ("red-blue-red-blue-red") → fascinating. Let them follow it.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they say the rule, or just predict? In what words?

## 24. The clap-stomp pattern (auditory)

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** None · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Sit facing your child. **"Watch and listen. Clap, stomp, clap, stomp, clap, stomp."** Do it slowly with sound. Then: **"Your turn — clap, stomp, clap, stomp …"** Let them continue. Then try a harder one: **"Clap, clap, stomp, clap, clap, stomp."**

### Magic prompt
> **"Can you make up your own pattern?"** This is the inversion — they invent, you follow. Most Pre-K kids love being the leader.

### What to listen for
- Audio/motor patterns are often easier than visual ones at this age.
- Can they extend? Can they invent? Two different skills.
- Invented patterns are sometimes irregular — that's information about their concept of "rule."

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they invent a pattern? What did it sound like?

## 25. Big-small-big-small (size)

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** Two sizes of any object — big and small blocks, big and small coins, plates and cups · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Lay out: **big, small, big, small, big.** **"What comes next?"** Then try: **big, big, small, big, big, small.**

### Magic prompt
> **"How is this pattern different from the red-blue one?"** (If you've done it.) Or simply: **"What's the rule here?"** Size patterns require attending to a different attribute than color.

### What to listen for
- Some kids handle color easily but get tangled in size. Same skill, different attribute — info about *which attributes they're tracking*.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Was size harder than color? Why?

## 26. Up-down-up-down (position)

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** A hand puppet or a stuffed animal · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Hold the puppet. **"Up, down, up, down, up …"** Move the puppet each time you say it. **"What comes next?"** Then: **"Up, up, down, up, up, down."**

### Magic prompt
> **"Can you do the pattern with me?"** They mirror. Then they lead, you follow.

### What to listen for
- Spatial-language patterns are useful precursors to graphing and number-line work later.
- Many kids can do this physically before they can describe it.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they lead well? What words did they use?

## 27. Three-color pattern (ABC)

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 9 objects in three colors · **Time:** 4–5 min

### Parent script
> Build: **red, blue, yellow, red, blue, yellow, red, blue …** **"What comes next?"** Three-element patterns are harder than two-element. Some Pre-K kids handle it, some don't yet.

### Magic prompt
> **"How did you know yellow comes after blue?"** Some will articulate the cycle; some will just feel it.

### What to listen for
- If they confidently predict, great.
- If they break the pattern (red after yellow instead of red after the cycle), they're tracking by "the color before" rather than the whole cycle. That's a discovery moment.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they handle three colors, or stick to thinking in twos?

## 28. The growing snake (growing pattern)

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 10+ identical blocks or coins · **Time:** 4–5 min

### Parent script
> Build a row of 1 block. Next to it, a row of 2. Next to that, a row of 3. **"Look at our snake — what do you notice?"** Build a row of 4. **"What comes next?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"How is each row different from the one before it?"** "One more" is the answer. Whether they articulate it or just continue building it.

### What to listen for
- They continue the pattern by adding one each time → strong intuitive grasp.
- They build a random next row → no pattern grasp yet, totally normal for Pre-K.
- "One more!" said aloud → developmental win.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they continue the +1 pattern? Could they say what was happening?

## 29. Pattern in the kitchen

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** Your kitchen · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Walk around the kitchen with your child. **"Can we find a pattern somewhere?"** Point to floor tiles, fabric patterns, the spice rack, anything that repeats. Each time you spot one, describe it: "Big tile, small tile, big tile, small tile."

### Magic prompt
> **"Can you find a pattern I haven't shown you?"** They become the pattern-spotter.

### What to listen for
- Real-world patterns are usually richer than constructed ones (3+ attributes at once).
- Finding their own pattern is the goal — even if it's not technically a perfect repeat.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Where did they find one? What did they call it?

## 30. Make your own

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** Mixed small objects (buttons, blocks, beans, coins — variety) · **Time:** 5–7 min

### Parent script
> Hand your child a small collection of mixed objects. **"Can you make a pattern? Any pattern you want."** When they do, ask: **"What's your rule? Can you keep it going?"**

### Magic prompt
> Then: **"Can you make a different pattern with the same things?"** Inventing a pattern is harder than continuing one. Inventing two different ones is harder still.

### What to listen for
- Invented patterns are sometimes just "arrangements" without a rule. That's fine — gently prompt: "What comes after the green block?"
- Two different patterns from the same objects → exceptional Pre-K abstraction.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> What patterns did they invent? Were they truly repeating, or just arrangements?

# Logic & Sorting (8)

## 31. Which one doesn't belong?

- **Strand:** logic_classification · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 4 objects with multiple defensible "doesn't belong" answers (e.g., 3 spoons + a fork; or yellow ball, yellow lemon, yellow duck, green apple) · **Time:** 4–5 min

### Parent script
> Lay out 4 objects. **"Which one doesn't belong?"** Whatever they pick, don't react. Ask: **"Why?"**

### Magic prompt
> After their first answer: **"Can you find a different one that doesn't belong, for a different reason?"** Every object can be the odd one out for some reason.

### What to listen for
- They flip and pick a different one for a different reason → abstract reasoning happening in real time.
- They invent an unusual category ("this one is the only one I don't like") → fine. They're using categories.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> What reasons did they give? Did the second one surprise you?

## 32. Sort by color

- **Strand:** logic_classification · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 10–12 objects in 2–3 colors (LEGO, buttons, beads, M&Ms) · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Mix the objects in a pile. **"Can you put all the same colors together?"** Let them sort.

### Magic prompt
> When done: **"How did you decide which go together?"** "By color" is the answer they may or may not articulate.

### What to listen for
- Sorting silently — they understand attribute, may not have the word.
- Sorting and naming colors — strong.

> **"Can you tell me where something is in this room — without pointing?"** Spatial language without gesture is harder.

## 62. The disappearing coin (odd/even intuition)

- **Strand:** math_games · **Level:** 1 · **Materials:** 6 coins or pebbles · **Time:** 5 min

- No pattern recognition → fine. Just play the game.

### Optional: what did your kid say?

# Working notes

**Authoring principles applied:**

- Every problem leans on one of the four magic prompts ("how do you know," "another way," "what if," "what did you notice") — usage roughly balanced across the set.
- Materials are everyday items — no specialty curriculum gear, no printables required.
- Time budgets are honest for Pre-K attention spans (3–8 min per problem).
- The optional capture field asks for *specifics* (what did they say, what did they do physically) — not generic ratings — so the corpus is mineable later.

**Known gaps to fix in iteration:**

- Pre-K problems don't currently encode a "what if the kid bails early" branch. Most parents will figure it out (stop, try again tomorrow) — but the parent script could state this explicitly: *"If your child loses interest after 90 seconds, that's information. Stop. Try a different one tomorrow."*
- Problems aren't yet tagged with which of the 4 magic prompts they primarily exercise — useful for the "structured corpus" goal once we port to schema.
- No accessibility considerations (e.g., kids with visual impairment, motor differences). Add as we test.

**Next:** field-test 4–6 of these (one per strand cluster), then port to schema with the `parent_script` field added to the Problem model.
