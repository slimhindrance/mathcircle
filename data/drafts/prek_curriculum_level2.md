# Pre-K curriculum — Level 2 (late Pre-K / K-bridge)

**62 problems** at `level: 2`. The conceptual step-up from Level 1:

| Strand | L1 → L2 progression |
|---|---|
| Number Sense | subitize 1–3 → 4–6; count to 5 → to 10 + backward; "more?" → "more by how many?" |
| Add/Sub Structures | "one more" → "two more"; recount → count-on; combine two small groups verbally |
| Missing Number | hide 1 of 3 → hide 1 of 5–7; one-step → two-step stories |
| Equality & Balance | match small piles → match 6–8; "are they equal?" → "if I take one from each, still equal?" |
| Patterns | ABAB → ABCABC, AABB, two-attribute; predict 1 ahead → predict 3 ahead; find the mistake |
| Logic & Sorting | sort by 1 attribute → 2 attributes; "is not" with 1 clue → 3 clues; rule from 4 examples → 5 + chained |
| Geometry & Spatial | upright shapes → rotated; vertical symmetry → horizontal; 2D → name 3D shapes |
| Measurement | compare 2 items → order 4–5; intuitive → estimate-then-measure |
| Counting Ways | 2 friends → 3 friends (6 arrangements); 1 outfit attribute → 2 attributes |
| Math Games | Nim-5 → Nim-7+; race-to-10 → race-to-15; even/odd 6 → 10 |

**Format (same as L1):**

- **Strand / Level / Materials / Time**
- **Parent script** — what the parent reads and does.
- **Magic prompt** — one of the four, deepened for the L2 cognitive demand.
- **What to listen for** — real late-Pre-K / pre-K-graduate behaviors.
- **Optional: what did your kid say?** — voluntary capture.

**Universal Pre-K rules apply:** parent-only screen, kitchen-table manipulatives, 4–10 min per problem, no "wrong" answers in the parent's voice.

# Number Sense (10)

## 1. Subitize 4 to 6

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 6 pebbles or raisins · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Put 4 pebbles on the table, briefly cover and reveal as before. **"How many?"** Then 5. Then 6. Mix the order. The arrangement matters — sometimes line them up, sometimes cluster them, sometimes spread them.

### Magic prompt
> **"How did you see it so fast?"** At Level 2 we're listening for *part-whole strategies*: "I saw 3 and 3" for 6, "4 and 1 more" for 5. That's a big developmental leap from raw subitizing.

### What to listen for
- "Three and three" / "two and four" → decomposing on the fly. Late-Pre-K marker.
- Counting one-by-one → fine, but slower. Try again with the same arrangement next session.
- Confident 4 and 5 but slower on 6 → totally normal; 6 is the upper subitizing edge at this age.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they decompose ("I saw 3 and 3") or count? Which numbers were instant vs. counted?

## 2. Count to 10 with matching

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 10 small objects · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Lay out 10 objects in a row. **"Let's count them — touch each one as we say a number."** Touch and count together to 10. Then: **"Your turn. I'll just listen."**

### Magic prompt
> **"What number comes after 7? And after 9?"** Naming the next number without counting from 1 is what we're working toward. Some kids will count internally first — fine.

### What to listen for
- Reliable 1–10 with one-to-one matching → solid.
- Skipping a number (1, 2, 3, 5...) → very common; gentle correction by counting together again.
- Knowing "after 7 is 8" without restarting → strong number-line intuition emerging.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they count up to 10 cleanly? Could they name the number after 7 without restarting?

---

## 3. Count backward from 10

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 10 raisins (or counters) on a plate · **Time:** 4–5 min

### Parent script
> Put 10 raisins out. **"We're going to eat them one at a time, counting backward. Ready? Ten … nine … eight …"** Eat (or move aside) one per number. Together at first; let them carry it solo on the second round.

### Magic prompt
> **"What comes before 5? What comes before 1?"** Backward counting is significantly harder than forward — predicting one *before* is a real cognitive lift.

### What to listen for
- 10 down to 5 clean, stumbles below → normal. Practice over weeks.
- "Before 1 is zero" → conceptual win.
- "Before 1 is nothing" or shrug → fine. Zero comes later for most.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> How far did they count backward cleanly? Their word for "before 1"?

## 4. Close call: 4 or 5?

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 12 buttons (or coins or beans) · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Make two piles: 4 on the left, 5 on the right. **"Which has more?"** Don't make it obvious — keep the piles visually similar. Repeat with 5 vs. 6, 6 vs. 7.

### Magic prompt
> **"How do you know it's more? Can you prove it?"** "Prove it" elicits the pairing or counting strategy. Just looking won't work when piles are close.

### What to listen for
- Pairs them off physically → mature strategy.
- Counts both piles → also mature.
- Visual judgment that turns out wrong → discovery moment. "Let's pair them up and see."

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Which strategy did they use? Did they realize visual judgment fails for close calls?

## 5. The next number game

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** None · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> **"I'll say a number, and you tell me the next one."** Start with 1 → 2. Then 5 → 6. Then 7 → 8. Then jump to 9 → 10. Then 0 → 1.

### Magic prompt
> **"And what comes two after 5?"** Skip-count by 1 twice — "5 → 6 → 7." Working memory and the number-line concept getting exercised.

### What to listen for
- Instant "after 5 is 6" → solid number-line internalization.
- Restarting from 1 ("one, two, three, four, five, six!") → developmental and fine.
- "Two after 5 is 7" without restarting → exceptional.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they name "next" without restarting? How about "two after"?

## 6. Five in many ways

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** Hands · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> **"Show me 5 fingers."** Most kids do one whole hand. **"That's one way! Can you show me 5 a different way?"** Encourage 4+1, 3+2. Then ask for 6, 7, 8 the same way (each across two hands).

### Magic prompt
> **"How many different ways can you show 5?"** Decomposing 5 systematically (4+1, 3+2, 2+3, 1+4, 5+0) is the foundation of number bonds — the K core concept.

### What to listen for
- One way → developmentally typical Pre-K.
- Two or three ways → late-Pre-K marker. Strong.
- Five ways (including 5+0 and asymmetric pairs) → K-ready thinking.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> How many different ways did they find? Which were intuitive vs. effortful?

## 7. Order them, least to most

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 4 plates with different small piles (e.g., 2, 5, 3, 7 pebbles) · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Lay out 4 plates with different amounts. **"Can you put them in order, smallest pile to biggest?"** Let them work physically.

### Magic prompt
> When they're done: **"How did you decide which was smallest? Which was biggest?"** Multi-way comparison is harder than pairwise.

### What to listen for
- Counting each pile, then ordering → strong process.
- Eyeballing for the extremes (biggest/smallest), counting the middle → very mature.
- Misorderings → discuss: "let's count them again, side by side."

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they count each? Did they get the ordering right? Where did mistakes happen?

## 8. Quick-look 5 and 6

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 6 stones (or buttons), a hand to cover · **Time:** 3 min

### Parent script
> Put 5 stones out, cover them, lift for 1 second, cover again. **"How many?"** Then 6. Mix it up. Vary the layout: line, cluster, scattered.

### Magic prompt
> **"Was it easier when they were in a line, or scattered?"** Layout dramatically affects subitizing. Noticing this is metacognition emerging.

### What to listen for
- Instant 5 in a line, slower scattered → typical pattern.
- Decomposing scattered ("I saw 3 and 2") → late-Pre-K.
- Frustration with 6 → 6 is the absolute upper limit of typical subitizing; OK to keep at 5.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did layout matter? What did they say about how they "saw" it?

## 9. More by how many?

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 10 pebbles · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Make 3 on the left, 5 on the right. **"Which is more? How many more?"** They might say "this one" without quantifying the difference. Press: "Yes, more — but how many *more*?"

### Magic prompt
> **"How could we figure out how many more?"** Pair off matching items; what's left over is the difference.

### What to listen for
- Pairs off and counts leftover → mature comparison strategy.
- Counts both and subtracts in their head → very advanced.
- "More, but I don't know how many more" → fine, suggest pairing.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they quantify the difference? What strategy did they use?

## 10. The "what comes after" hop

- **Strand:** number_sense · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** Floor tape or chalk marks for a number line 0–10, or just imagine · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Mark 0–10 on the floor with tape. **"Hop to 4."** They hop. **"Now hop forward two — where are you?"** Count the hops aloud. Then back two: "where are you now?" Try other starts.

### Magic prompt
> **"What if you hop forward three from 6?"** Use the physical number line — they should walk it and check. Number-line reasoning grounded in the body.

### What to listen for
- Hopping accurately by count → embodied number-line forming.
- "From 6, three hops is 9" without hopping → mental number line developing.
- Going past 10 → discuss: "what comes after 10?" Eleven. New territory.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they need to physically hop, or could they reason without it?

# Add & Subtract Structures (5)

## 11. Two more, two less

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 10 small toys · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Line up 3 bears. **"How many? Now add two more."** Pause for them to do it. **"How many now?"** Then take 2 away. **"And now?"** Continue with different starts.

### Magic prompt
> **"You started with 3, added 2, and got 5. How did you know it was 5?"** Listen for counting all vs. counting on vs. just knowing.

### What to listen for
- Recounts all → still typical at Pre-K.
- "Three… four, five" (counts on by 2) → emerging late-Pre-K strategy.
- "Three plus two is five" without counting → K-ready.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> How did they get to 5 from 3+2? Their actual method?

---

## 12. Count on from here

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** Two hands · **Time:** 3–4 min

### Parent script
> Hold up 4 fingers. **"This is 4. Don't count them — just remember: 4."** Then hold up 2 more on the other hand. **"How many altogether?"** The goal is for them to say "five, six" rather than "one, two, three, four, five, six."

### Magic prompt
> **"You didn't start at one this time. You started at four. Why?"** Naming the strategy — "I already knew it was 4 so I started there" — is a real metacognitive win.

### What to listen for
- Counts on from 4 → late-Pre-K marker. Excellent.
- Still starts from 1 → fine; the strategy comes with practice.
- Says "5, 6" out loud → they're vocalizing the count-on. Encourage.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they count on, or did they restart? Their words for the strategy?

---

## 13. Take from 7

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 7 pebbles or raisins · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Put 7 pebbles out. Count them together. **"Take away 3."** They remove 3. **"How many are left?"** Then try: start with 7, take 5. Then 7, take 1.

### Magic prompt
> **"You started with 7 and took 3, and got 4. Is there a way to know that without recounting?"** Some kids will spot the pattern (7 → 6, 5, 4, three steps back).

### What to listen for
- Recounts the leftover → standard.
- Counts down from 7: "six, five, four" while taking → strong subtraction-as-counting-back.
- "Seven take three is four" without doing it physically → exceptional.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they have to recount, or work it out by counting back?

---

## 14. Combine two groups

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 8 small objects · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Make two piles: 3 on the left, 4 on the right. **"How many altogether?"** Let them work — they might push together, count both, count on.

### Magic prompt
> **"Is there a way to know without counting them all?"** Listen for counting-on: "four… five, six, seven." Or known facts: "three plus four is seven."

### What to listen for
- Pushes them together and counts all 7 → typical.
- Counts on from 4 → late-Pre-K.
- "Three and four is seven" → ready for K.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> What strategy did they pick? Did it change across the rounds?

## 15. Seven in many ways

- **Strand:** add_sub_structures · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 7 pennies · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Show 7 pennies. **"Can you split these into two piles? Any way you like."** They split. Note the split: e.g. 3 + 4. **"Can you split them a different way?"** Continue until they're tired.

### Magic prompt
> **"How many different ways can we split 7?"** Don't expect them to find all 6; finding 3–4 is great. Number bonds for 7 in physical form.

### What to listen for
- Two different splits → developmental win.
- Treating 3+4 and 4+3 as the same → mature.
- Treating them as different → also fine; commutativity is a later insight.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> How many splits did they find? Did they spot 3+4 vs 4+3 as same/different?

# Missing Number Stories (3)

## 16. Under the cup, with 7

- **Strand:** missing_number_stories · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 2 opaque cups, 7 pennies · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Show 7 pennies. Have them count to 7. Split: some under cup A, some under cup B (you choose: say 3 and 4). Lift cup A: 3. **"How many under the other cup? How do you know?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"How did you figure that out?"** The missing-addend logic — 7 total, 3 visible, must be 4 hidden — is a key K concept emerging early.

### What to listen for
- "Because 3 and 4 is 7" → reasoning structurally.
- "Because I counted 7 and saw 3, so it's the rest" → counting-based.
- A guess → reveal, recount together, try again with a different split.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Reasoning or guessing? What reasoning, if any?

---

## 17. Two-step story

- **Strand:** missing_number_stories · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 8 small toys, one box or basket · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> **"There are 5 ducks in the pond. Two more ducks fly in."** Have them count: 7. **"Then 3 ducks fly away."** They count: 4. **"How many ducks are left?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"Did anyone count? Or did you just know each time?"** Two-step problems require holding intermediate state — a working-memory exercise.

### What to listen for
- Acts it out physically → strong support strategy.
- Tracks counts in their head → late-Pre-K to early-K.
- Loses track → totally fine; redo with smaller starts (3 + 2 - 1).

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they track two steps? Where did they lose the thread, if anywhere?

---

## 18. Backwards story

- **Strand:** missing_number_stories · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 6 toy animals · **Time:** 4–5 min

### Parent script
> **"There were some animals at the park. Three went home. Now there are 4 left. How many were there at the start?"** Hard. Have them act it out — start with 4 (the leftover), add 3 back.

### Magic prompt
> **"You worked backwards. What did you have to figure out?"** Reasoning from the result is the seed of algebra.

### What to listen for
- Acting it out and recounting → strong.
- "Seven, because 4 plus 3 is 7" → exceptional.
- Stuck → no shame. This is hard. Try with 2 + 2 = 4 instead.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they reason backwards? What made it click, or where did they get stuck?

# Equality & Balance (4)

## 19. Match my pile of 8

- **Strand:** equality_balance · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 16 small objects · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Make a pile of 8 in front of you. **"Can you make a pile the same size as mine?"** Let them work. Then ask how they know.

### Magic prompt
> **"How can you be sure they're the same without counting?"** Pairing off — lining one of theirs against one of yours — is the equality-as-relation insight.

### What to listen for
- Counting up to 8 in their pile → solid strategy.
- Pairing one-to-one → equality concept very strong.
- "About the same" by eyeball with 8 each → push for verification.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Counted or paired? Confident in their answer?

---

## 20. Three on each side

- **Strand:** equality_balance · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** Homemade balance (coat hanger + cups) or kitchen scale, 6 identical small objects · **Time:** 5–6 min

### Parent script
> Put 3 pennies on one side of the balance. **"How many on the other side to balance it?"** They guess — let them add one at a time. **"Tell me when it's balanced."**

### Magic prompt
> **"Could it be balanced with fewer? With more?"** The "same amount of stuff" idea is foundational; will fail interestingly when objects have different weights, which is fine to explore.

### What to listen for
- Adds three one at a time → strong iteration.
- Adds two and notices it's "not enough" → strong visual/physical feedback.
- "It has to be three because it's three on the other side" → equality reasoning.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they articulate why it balanced at three?

---

## 21. Take one from each

- **Strand:** equality_balance · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 12 pebbles · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Make two equal piles: 5 and 5. **"Same?"** Yes. **"Now I take one from this pile."** Take one. **"Are they still the same?"** Then: put it back. Take one from each. **"Now?"** Still same!

### Magic prompt
> **"Why is it still the same after I took one from each? But not after I took one from only one?"** This is the "balanced operations" idea — the foundation of solving equations someday.

### What to listen for
- "Because they both lost one" → strong relational thinking.
- Confused → totally fine. Demonstrate with smaller piles (2 and 2).
- Aha moment → big developmental win, often visible.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they get the "take from each = still equal" idea? In what words?

---

## 22. Make them fair

- **Strand:** equality_balance · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 11 raisins, 2 plates · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Plate A has 6 raisins. Plate B has 5. **"They're not fair. Can you make them fair?"** They might split the extra (impossible without breaking), move one over (making 5 and 6 → uneven again), or eat one. All teach something.

### Magic prompt
> **"What did you have to do? Is there another way to make them fair?"** Multiple solutions exist (one eaten, one set aside, one split).

### What to listen for
- Sets one aside → understands fairness as equality.
- Moves one from A to B → ends up with 5 and 6 again. Discovery: "still not fair!"
- Wants to break/split → great, but discuss: "raisins don't really split. What else could we do?"

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> What approaches did they try? Any failed attempts that led to insight?

# Patterns (8)

## 23. ABCABC three-color

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 12 objects in 3 colors · **Time:** 4–5 min

### Parent script
> Build a pattern: **red, blue, yellow, red, blue, yellow, red, blue, ...** **"What comes next?"** They predict. Continue: **"And after that?"** Then a different one: **red, red, blue, red, red, blue, ...**

### Magic prompt
> **"How would you describe this pattern to someone who couldn't see it?"** Articulating a three-element rule in words is harder than two.

### What to listen for
- Confident prediction across the cycle → solid grasp.
- Predicts correctly but can't say the rule → typical. Words follow understanding.
- Verbalizes "red blue yellow keeps coming again" → strong articulation.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they describe a 3-color rule in words?

---

## 24. AABB doubled pattern

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 12 objects in 2 colors · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Build: **red, red, blue, blue, red, red, blue, blue, ...** **"What comes next?"** AABB feels different from ABAB. Many kids predict ABAB by reflex; let them and see.

### Magic prompt
> **"How is this pattern different from red-blue-red-blue?"** Comparing two patterns surfaces the structural difference.

### What to listen for
- Predicts "red, red" → caught the doubling.
- Predicts "blue" → reflex from ABAB. Reveal "red, red" and discuss.
- Builds their own AABB → exceptional.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they notice the doubling? Could they make their own AABB?

## 25. Big-red, small-blue (two attributes)

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** Objects varying in size AND color — e.g., big red, small blue, big red, small blue ... · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Build the pattern, mixing two attributes that change together. **"What comes next?"** They predict — both size and color have to be right.

### Magic prompt
> **"What two things change every time?"** Surfacing the two-attribute structure is a real abstraction step.

### What to listen for
- Gets size right, color wrong → tracking one attribute.
- Both right → tracking two. Late-Pre-K marker.
- Articulates "it goes big red, then small blue" → strong verbal grasp.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they track both attributes? Did they verbalize the rule?

## 26. Predict three ahead

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 8 objects in 2 colors · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Build: **red, blue, red, blue, red.** **"What comes next?"** They say blue. **"And after that?"** Red. **"And after that?"** Blue. Three predictions deep.

### Magic prompt
> **"How could you know the eighth one without doing it one at a time?"** Some kids will start counting (1-red, 2-blue, 3-red, 4-blue) — that's the pattern-extending strategy at work.

### What to listen for
- Three correct predictions → solid.
- Loses track at the second or third → working memory limit. Build it physically while they predict.
- Computes "the 8th is blue" by rule → exceptional.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> How far ahead could they predict? Any signs of "rule-based" thinking vs. step-by-step?

## 27. Growing snake

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 15+ identical blocks · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Build rows of blocks: 1, then next to it 2, then 3, then 4. **"Look at our snake. What's the rule?"** Then ask them to build the 5th and 6th rows.

### Magic prompt
> **"How many in the 7th row? Can you tell without building it?"** Counting forward through a growing pattern is real mathematical reasoning.

### What to listen for
- "Seven, because it goes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7" → strong.
- Builds it first to check → also strong, more grounded.
- "One more than the row before" articulated → captured the rule.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they articulate the +1 rule? Could they predict ahead?

## 28. Find the mistake

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 10 objects in 2 colors · **Time:** 3–4 min

### Parent script
> Build a pattern but slip in a mistake: **red, blue, red, blue, blue, red, blue, red.** (Note the two blues in a row.) **"There's a mistake in this pattern. Can you find it?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"How did you know that was the mistake?"** They have to internalize the rule to spot the violation.

### What to listen for
- Finds the two-blues → strong pattern internalization.
- Points to a different spot → fine, ask "why?" — they might have a different mental model.
- Can't find it → walk through with them: "red, blue, red, blue, blue — wait."

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they spot the mistake? Their explanation?

## 29. Clap-stomp into blocks

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** Blocks in 2 colors · **Time:** 4–5 min

### Parent script
> Do a clap-stomp pattern with sound: **clap, stomp, clap, stomp, clap, stomp.** **"Can you build that same pattern with these blocks? Use red for clap and blue for stomp."**

### Magic prompt
> **"What if the pattern were clap-clap-stomp? How would the blocks look?"** Translating between modalities (audio → visual) tests structural understanding.

### What to listen for
- Builds correct color-coded pattern → cross-modal pattern transfer. Strong.
- Confuses the order of audio events → fine, repeat the sound pattern.
- Inventing their own audio→visual mapping → bonus.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Could they translate audio to visual? Any inventions of their own?

## 30. Pattern of patterns

- **Strand:** patterns · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 12+ blocks in 2 colors · **Time:** 5–6 min

### Parent script
> Build: **red, blue, red, blue,** [pause and gesture] **then,** **red, red, blue, red, red, blue.** Two different patterns next to each other. **"Did the pattern change? Where?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"Why did the pattern change? Can you build one with three different patterns in a row?"** Recognizing that "rule" itself can change is meta-pattern thinking.

### What to listen for
- Identifies the switch → strong pattern attention.
- Builds their own with multiple sections → exceptional.
- Just keeps the first pattern going → typical; gently point out the change.

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Did they notice the switch? Could they make their own multi-section pattern?

# Logic & Sorting (8)

## 31. Sort by two attributes

- **Strand:** logic_classification · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** Buttons or blocks in 2 colors AND 2 sizes (4 categories — big red, big blue, small red, small blue) · **Time:** 5 min

### Parent script
> Mix them up. **"Can you sort these so that things that are the same go together?"** Don't say which attribute. See what they pick.

### Magic prompt
> **"You sorted them by [color]. Could you also sort them by [size]? Could you sort them by both at once?"** Two-attribute sorting (4 categories) is a real cognitive step.

### What to listen for
- Sorts by one attribute first → typical.
- Discovers four categories on their own → exceptional.
- Confuses the four-way sort → fine, demo with smaller set (4 items).

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Which attribute did they pick first? Could they handle 4 categories?

## 32. Three "is not" clues

- **Strand:** logic_classification · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 5 different small objects · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script
> Lay out 5 items. **"I'm thinking of one. It's not red. It's not soft. It's not the biggest one."** Three clues. **"Which one am I thinking of?"**

### Magic prompt
> **"Which clue helped most?"** Reasoning about clue value is metacognition.

### What to listen for
- Eliminates step by step → strong logical thinking.
- Jumps to an answer without eliminating → ask "why?"; they may have used a shortcut.
- Confused by negative clues → fine, restate as positive ("it's blue").

### Optional: what did your kid say?
> Which clue did they say helped? Did they eliminate or jump?

## 33. Rule from five examples

- **Strand:** logic_classification · **Level:** 2 · **Materials:** 8 items, pre-sorted: 5 in pile A (all blue), 3 in pile B (not blue) · **Time:** 4 min

### Parent script

### What to listen for

# Working notes

This Level 2 set targets the late-Pre-K / Pre-K-to-K-bridge band:

- **Number sense:** subitize to 6, count to 10 + backward, compare close numbers, decompose 5 and 7, count-on
- **Patterns:** 3-color (ABC), doubled (AABB), two-attribute (size+color), predict 3 ahead, find the mistake, cross-modal translation
- **Logic:** 2-attribute sort, 3 "is not" clues, rule from 5 examples, sort same set 3 ways, intersection (both A AND B)
- **Geometry:** shape constancy under rotation, horizontal symmetry, 3D shape names (sphere/cube/cylinder/cone), multi-constraint spatial reasoning
- **Add/sub:** count-on, take-from-7, 2-step stories, decompose 7
- **Equality:** match 8, take-from-each invariance, fairness puzzles
- **Counting ways:** 3-arrangement (6 ways), 2×2 outfits
- **Math games:** Nim-7, race-to-15, comparison-to-7, even/odd to 10

**When the K-2 adaptive engine sees a Pre-K child at Level 2:**
- All ten strands tracked independently — kid may be Level 2 in Patterns and still Level 1 in Geometry.
- Parent rating still drives Level adjustment within Pre-K (Level 1 ↔ Level 2).
- The "ready for Kindergarten" prompt fires when the child is at Level 2 in ≥7 of 10 strands with no recent "too hard" pattern — meaning they've meaningfully engaged with the deeper material, not just clicked through 8 starter problems.
